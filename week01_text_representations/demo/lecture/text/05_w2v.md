# w2v · Word2vec: skip-gram, CBOW, and fastText

## w2v-p1

One-hot gave every word its own axis and no similarity at all. **Word2vec** (Mikolov et al. 2013) gives every word a short dense vector — a hundred or three hundred numbers — and learns those numbers from raw text, so that words used alike end up close. It is the distributional hypothesis turned into a prediction task, in two mirror-image versions.


## sg-p1

**Skip-gram** takes one word — the *centre* — and predicts the words around it. A window, say two words on each side, slides over the text, and every (centre, context) pair inside it is one training example: `oil prices rise today` with the centre `prices` gives (prices, oil), (prices, rise), (prices, today).

The model is two matrices. $W_{in}$, of size |V| × d, holds one row per word. The centre word comes in as a one-hot vector, and multiplying it by $W_{in}$ just picks out its row — that row, $v_c$, *is* the word's current vector, and it is called $h$ while it passes through the network. $W_{out}$, of size d × |V|, holds one column $u_w$ per word. Multiplying $h$ by $W_{out}$ gives a score $u_w · v_c$ for every word of the vocabulary, and a softmax turns the scores into a distribution over possible context words:

$P(o | c) = \frac{\mathrm{exp}(u_o · v_c)}{\munder{∑}{w} \mathrm{exp}(u_w · v_c)}$

We're gonna work in pairs: one word that comes in, one word that comes out. For skip-gram there going to be the same centre word and $n$ different outputs as a second word in the pair. So, the model will see one input and learn to give $n$ different outputs. The loss of one pair is the cross-entropy against the context word that was actually there, $−\mathrm{log} P(o | c)$: a |V|-way classification, the same softmax as any classifier, with the neighbouring word as the label.

> **E**, the softmax output, is compared with the one-hot vector of the word that actually stood in the window — `prices` for the pair (oil, prices) — and the loss is $−\mathrm{log}$ of the probability E gives to it. The label is free: the text itself says which words were neighbours. That is why word2vec needs no annotation at all.

## train-p1

Training is plain gradient descent on that loss, one pair at a time. Both matrices start as **small random numbers** — nothing about language is put in. For a pair (c, o), the gradient moves the centre's row $v_c$ towards $u_o$ and away from the columns of the other words in proportion to how much probability they took, and moves $u_o$ towards $v_c$. Two words that keep appearing next to the same context words receive the same pushes and drift together: the distributional hypothesis, executed by the optimiser. The derivation of these gradients, step by step, is in [Stanford CS224N, Lecture 2: Word Vector Representations — word2vec](https://youtu.be/ERibwqs9p38).

The prediction task itself is a **pretext**. Nobody needs a model that guesses the neighbours of a word, and it never guesses them well. What we want is the by-product: the rows of $W_{in}$.

> The training target is the neighbour: given the centre, a softmax over the vocabulary scored against the word that was really in the window. Its accuracy is poor and irrelevant. What we keep is $W_{in}$ — one vector per word, shaped by millions of these predictions — and the task is thrown away. Next-word prediction is the language model of the next lecture: a related conditional, a different goal. Nobody labels synonyms; the point of the construction is that the only supervision is raw text.
> miss: if "the next word": close family, different conditional. Skip-gram looks both ways inside a window and never models a sequence.

## cbow-p1

**CBOW** — continuous bag of words — is the mirror image: the context predicts the centre. The rows of all context words are taken from $W_{in}$ and *averaged* into one vector $h$; their order is lost on the way, hence "bag of words". $h$ goes through $W_{out}$ and a softmax over the vocabulary, now scored against the centre word. One window is one training example, where skip-gram made one example per context word.

The difference shows up on frequency. CBOW is faster and smooths over frequent words. Skip-gram gives every occurrence of a rare word its own updates, one per neighbour, and represents rare words better (Mikolov et al. 2013): for CBOW there going to be only one run with one central word, while for Skip-Gram we'll see it $n$ times.

## q-cbow-vs · pick

What is the real difference between CBOW and skip-gram?

- [x] the direction: CBOW predicts the centre from the averaged context, skip-gram predicts each context word from the centre
- [ ] CBOW keeps the order of the context words, skip-gram does not
- [ ] CBOW has no output matrix
- [ ] skip-gram needs labelled data, CBOW does not

> The direction of prediction, and with it the number of examples. CBOW averages the context into one vector and predicts one word per window; skip-gram turns one window into several (centre, context) pairs. Both use the same two matrices, both learn from raw text only, both keep $W_{in}$ at the end. Neither keeps order inside the window: CBOW averages it away, skip-gram treats every neighbour as a separate example.
> miss: if "CBOW keeps the order": the bag in its name says the opposite. The average of `oil prices` and of `prices oil` is the same vector.

## neg-p1

One practical problem remains. The softmax over the whole vocabulary costs one dot product per vocabulary word — tens of thousands — for every single training pair. The same paper replaced it with a cheaper task, **negative sampling**: for each real pair (c, o), draw k random words (five to twenty, sampled by frequency) and train a yes/no classifier — the sigmoid of the dot product — to say *yes* to the real pair and *no* to the random ones:

$\mathrm{log} σ(u_o · v_c) + \munder{∑}{k} \mathrm{log} σ(−u_k · v_c)$

The two matrices stay; only the loss changes, from one |V|-way softmax to k + 1 sigmoids.

## q-w2v-neg · pick

Negative sampling turns the training task into

- [x] a yes/no decision per pair: was this (centre, word) seen together, or was the word drawn at random?
- [ ] the same |V|-way softmax, computed faster
- [ ] a regression onto co-occurrence counts
- [ ] dropping the most frequent words

> A binary task. Instead of normalising over the whole vocabulary, the model scores one real pair and a handful of random ones with a sigmoid of the dot product between the centre's row of $W_{in}$ and the word's column of $W_{out}$. That is also why $W_{out}$ is a legitimate embedding: it is trained by the same dot products as $W_{in}$, from the other side.
> miss: if "dropping frequent words": that is subsampling, a separate trick in the same paper, applied to the *input* text. Negative sampling is about the loss.

## ft-p1

Word2vec has one row per word of the training vocabulary and nothing for anything else, and it treats `walk`, `walks`, `walked` as three unrelated rows. **fastText** (Bojanowski et al. 2017) keeps the skip-gram objective and changes what the vector of a word is: the sum of the vectors of its **character n-grams**. The word is wrapped in boundary marks and cut into pieces — for `where` with n = 3: `<wh`, `whe`, `her`, `ere`, `re>`, plus the whole word `<where>`; by default n runs from 3 to 6. Every piece has its own learned vector, and the word's vector is their sum.

Two things follow. Pieces are shared between words, so `walked` and `walking` share most of their parameters and rare forms borrow from frequent ones. And a word never seen in training still gets a vector — the sum of the pieces it shares with seen words. To keep memory bounded, n-grams are hashed into a fixed number of buckets, two million by default; two different n-grams can land in one bucket, and that collision is accepted.

## keep-p1

When training ends, the task is thrown away and $W_{in}$ is kept: a table with one vector per word of the vocabulary — or, for fastText, a table of n-gram vectors that yields a vector for any string — learned from raw text with no labels at all.

## q-w2v-use · write

Now you have $W_{in}$: 30 000 rows of 100 numbers, trained on raw text with no labels. What could you solve with it as it is — and what would you still need labels for?

- nearest neighbours: synonyms, query expansion, grouping misspellings and variants
- a document vector by pooling, as the input of a classifier — the head still needs labels
- word arithmetic and the bias audit that comes with it (and the catch: the nearest vector to king − man + woman is king)
- initialising the embedding layer of a larger model
- clustering words or documents without any labels

> Without labels: everything that is a *distance*. Nearest neighbours — synonyms, spelling variants, query expansion for search; clustering of words, and of documents through pooled vectors; the arithmetic, `king − man + woman`, and its uncomfortable twin, `doctor − man + woman`, which is a bias audit of the corpus (Bolukbasi et al. 2016; and read Nissim et al. 2020 before you trust the demo — with the input words kept, the nearest vector to `king − man + woman` is `king` itself). As an initialisation: the embedding layer of any later model can start from these rows instead of from random.
>
> With labels: any decision. Classification, regression, routing — the embedding is only the input, and a classifier on top of it still has to be trained. What the labels buy is cheaper: 100 numbers per word are a far smaller input than 30 000, so a few hundred examples can train the head where a bag of words needed thousands.
> miss: if the list is only "classification": that is a head, not a use of the embedding. The embedding is the part that works before anyone labels anything.
