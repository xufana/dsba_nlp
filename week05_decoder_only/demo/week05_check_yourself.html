Check yourself

Attention, the encoder, the decoder — and the parts of the code that nobody puts in the paper: padding, masks, collators, the cache. Every question has an answer that fits in a few sentences. If yours doesn't, that is the thing to reread.

01Take a decoder block and remove cross-attention. What is the one remaining difference from an encoder block?

The mask. Same embeddings, same self-attention, same FFN, same residuals and LayerNorms; the encoder lets every position read every other, the decoder blanks out everything to the right of the query. That is the whole of "encoder vs decoder" at the level of the block.

02Why does the KV cache exist at all? What guarantees that the keys and values of earlier positions do not change when a new token is appended?

The causal mask. Position i only ever attends to positions ≤ i, so nothing appended on its right can reach it in any layer; its hidden state, and therefore its K and V in every layer, are identical at step t and step t+1. Recomputing them is pure waste, so the model stores them and adds one row per new token.

The same argument says why an encoder cannot have such a cache: appending a token changes every position's state, because every position reads it.

03A batch holds sentences of different lengths. What does padding physically do, and why is the [PAD] token by itself not enough?

Padding makes the batch a rectangle: every sequence is extended to the same length so it can be one tensor. On its own that is only a shape fix — the model still sees [PAD] as a token: other positions attend to it, its vector enters a mean pool, its position gets a label and a loss.

The attention_mask is the second half: it sets the padded keys to −∞ before the softmax, so no real token can read them. Then, separately, the loss must be told to skip those positions (−100 in the labels) and any pooling must divide by the real length, not the padded one.

watch for"the tokenizer handles it" — the tokenizer returns the mask; whether it is passed to the model and the pooling is your code.
04Attention mask and causal mask — the same thing? How do the two combine inside a decoder?

Two different masks with two different jobs. The causal mask is the same for every sequence: a lower triangle that hides the future. The attention (padding) mask comes with the batch: it hides the positions that are not real tokens. In a decoder they are combined with a logical AND — a key is visible only if it is in the past and real.

05Pad on the left or on the right? For BERT and for GPT the answer is different. Why?

For an encoder with a padding mask it does not matter: every real token sees every other real token either way, and pooling is over the mask. For generation it matters, because the next token is read from the last position of the batch tensor — logits[:, -1]. Right-pad, and that position is a [PAD] for every sequence shorter than the longest; the model predicts what follows a pad. Left-pad, and the last position is the last real token of every sequence.

06What does a transformer layer spend its parameters on — attention or the FFN? And what does each of the two sublayers do?

The FFN, by about two to one: attention has four d×d matrices (Q, K, V, O), the FFN has d×4d and 4d×d, so 4d² against 8d². They do different jobs. Attention is the only sublayer that moves information between positions; the FFN is applied to each position independently and processes what attention gathered. Most of what looks like stored knowledge — "Paris is the capital of" → "France" — is recoverable from FFN weights, which is why the FFN is where model editing and most of the parameter count live.

watch forattributing everything to attention. Attention is the routing; the FFN is most of the compute.
07Why are padded label positions set to −100 and not to 0?

Because 0 is a real class. In token classification it is usually O; in language modelling it is a real vocabulary id. The loss would learn to predict that class on every pad and the metric would count them as correct. −100 is PyTorch's default ignore_index in CrossEntropyLoss: those positions contribute neither to the loss nor to its normalisation. The whole HF ecosystem uses the same number for exactly that reason, so the collators, the models and the metrics agree without configuration.

08Why can BERT not generate text, even though it predicts a word at every [MASK]?

Because what it models is p(x_i | everything else), with the right context given. Generation needs p(x_t | x_<t) — a distribution over the next token when the future is not known — and the chain of those is a factorisation of the joint p(x). Bidirectional conditionals do not chain into a joint; you can iteratively mask and fill, but the result is not a sample from any consistent distribution, and the model was never trained on inputs that are half missing.

watch for"it just wasn't trained for that". The obstacle is the conditioning, not the data.
09The one-token shift for a causal LM — who does it: the collator, the model, or you?

In transformers, the model. GPT2LMHeadModel (and every other *ForCausalLM) takes labels equal to input_ids and shifts internally: logits at position t are scored against the label at t+1. DataCollatorForLanguageModeling(mlm=False) therefore only copies input_ids into labels and replaces pads with −100. Shift once more yourself, and the model is trained to predict the token after the next one.

10Why does dividing the scores by √d_k matter — does the model break without it, or just train a little worse?

It can break. If the entries of q and k are of unit variance, their dot product has variance d_k; at d_k = 64 the scores are spread eight times wider than the vectors. A softmax over scores in the tens is nearly one-hot, and the gradient of a saturated softmax is nearly zero — the attention weights stop learning. Dividing by √d_k puts the scores back at unit variance, so the softmax starts out soft. It is a temperature, chosen so that the initial temperature is 1.

watch for"for normalisation" with nothing after it. Normalise what, and what goes wrong otherwise.
11What does a collator do that Dataset.__getitem__ does not — and why can padding not be done inside the dataset?

The dataset returns one example and knows nothing about the others. Padding is a property of the batch: the width is the longest sequence in it. The collator is the function that receives the list of examples and turns it into one set of tensors — pads to the batch maximum, builds the attention mask, pads the labels with −100, and for MLM does the masking. Padding inside the dataset means padding every example to the global maximum, which for AG News is several times the mean length — most of the arithmetic would be spent on pads.

12The word Zwingmann becomes three WordPiece tokens. Which of them carries the NER tag, and how do you get back to words at inference time?

The first piece carries the word's tag; the continuations get −100 and are skipped by the loss. At inference you read the prediction at the first piece of every word and ignore the rest; tokenizer.word_ids() gives the word index of every piece, in both directions. Copying the tag to every piece looks harmless and is not: to anything that reads BIO, B-PER B-PER B-PER is three persons in a row.

13What is stored in the KV cache, what is not, and why?

Per layer, the key and value vectors of every past position: two tensors of shape [layers, heads, T, d_head]. Not the queries — a query is needed only once, at the position that asks, and never again. Not the hidden states either: the next layer's input at past positions is not needed, because past positions are never recomputed; only their K and V are read.

The size is what makes it matter: for a 7B model at 4 096 tokens the cache is in the gigabytes, and a GPU serving many requests runs out of memory for caches long before it runs out of arithmetic.

Step t: the new token computes its own Q, K, V; its K and V are appended; its Q reads all K and V rows in this layer. Rows 1…t−1 are read, never recomputed.
14Remove the positional embeddings from an encoder. What differs between its output on "dog bites man" and on "man bites dog"?

Nothing except the order of the rows. Self-attention is a function of the set of input vectors: permute the inputs and the outputs are permuted the same way, with the same values. Without positions the encoder is a bag of words with context mixing, and no amount of training gives it word order, because word order is not in its input. That is the only reason positional information has to be added at all.

watch for"it will pick up the order from context". There is no context that carries order.
15GPT-2 has no pad token. You set pad_token = eos_token. Where does that bite?

Two places. In training, the collator replaces every pad_token_id in the labels with −100 — including the real end-of-text token, so the model is never taught to stop; a common reason for fine-tunes that generate until max_new_tokens. Fix: mask the pads through the attention mask, not through the token id, or add a real pad token and resize the embeddings. At inference, with generate, the same token in the mask is fine as long as the attention mask is passed — the model never reads the pads.

16Left padding and positions: what happens to position_ids if nobody shifts them?

The first real token gets position 5 instead of 0 (with five pads), and the whole sequence is offset. With learned absolute positions the model sees a sentence starting mid-way through its position table; the damage is small for short offsets and grows with them. generate handles it: it builds position_ids as the cumulative sum of the attention mask minus one, so pads do not count. A hand-written forward pass does not, unless you pass position_ids yourself. Models with rotary positions have the same issue: the rotation angle is the position, and it must skip the pads.

17Why is the first generated token slow and every following one fast? What are the two phases called, and what does each one hit?

Prefill runs the whole prompt in one pass: T positions through every layer, one large matrix multiply per weight — the GPU is doing arithmetic, and the phase is compute-bound. Decode runs one position per step: every weight matrix is read from memory to multiply one vector, so each step is limited by how fast the weights (and the cache) can be streamed, not by FLOPs — memory-bandwidth-bound. That is why batching many requests together in decode is nearly free in time and why "tokens per second" for a single stream is a bandwidth number.

18Three collators — ForLanguageModeling, ForSeq2Seq, ForTokenClassification. What does each one do to the labels?

DataCollatorForLanguageModeling builds the labels itself: with mlm=True it picks the 15 % positions, applies 80/10/10, and sets every other label to −100; with mlm=False it copies input_ids and masks the pads. DataCollatorForSeq2Seq receives labels (the target ids) and pads them with −100 to the batch width — and, given the model, builds decoder_input_ids by shifting them right with the start token. DataCollatorForTokenClassification receives one label per token and pads that list with −100. Three behaviours, one shared convention: pads in the labels are −100, pads in the inputs are the pad id.

19Truncating to 512 tokens is acceptable for classification and not for extractive QA. Why, and what do you do instead?

For classification the label is a property of the whole text and the first 512 tokens usually carry it. For QA the label is a position; cut the paragraph and the answer may be in the part you threw away — the label is then unrepresentable, not just harder. The standard fix is a sliding window: the paragraph is split into overlapping chunks (stride), each paired with the full question, the answer is labelled in the chunk that contains it and as "no answer" in the others, and at prediction time the best span across all chunks wins. In the tokenizer that is return_overflowing_tokens=True plus offset_mapping to map spans back to characters.

One long context, windows of a fixed width with a stride. The answer is inside window 2 only; windows 1 and 3 are trained to say "no answer" (pointing at [CLS]).
20Why can a decoder-only model classify, while an encoder-only model cannot write an answer? What exactly is asymmetric?

Any task can be phrased as "continue this text" — a label is a word, an answer is a string — so a decoder with a vocabulary head covers every task by putting the task in the prefix. Not every task can be phrased as "put a label on a position": a head has to enumerate its answers, and an answer that is longer than the input, or nests, or is a string that is not in the text, has no slot to go in. The asymmetry is the output space: the vocabulary head is universal, a classification head is not. The price of universality is one forward pass per output token, and no guarantee about the output's shape.

21Why group examples by length before batching? What is the price?

With random batches the width is the longest example in the batch, and for skewed length distributions that is far above the mean — most positions are pads, and attention is quadratic in the width. Sorting into buckets of similar length (group_by_length in the Trainer) makes each batch nearly pad-free, typically a 2–3× speed-up on text with varied lengths. The price is that batches are no longer i.i.d. samples: a batch of short texts and a batch of long ones may differ systematically, which slightly increases gradient noise. Shuffling the buckets, not the examples, is the usual compromise.

22Is key j in the second layer still "word j"?

No. After one layer, position j's vector is the token plus whatever the first layer's heads gathered from its neighbours; its second-layer key is a projection of that mixed vector. So a second-layer head attending to position j is attending to "word j in context", and reading a deep attention map as word-to-word alignment is a mistake. Layer-one maps are often legible (previous token, next token, self); deeper ones are not, and it is not because they are noisy.

23Does a transformer layer replace the representation or add to it? What follows for how you read the layers?

It adds. Every sublayer's output is x + f(x); the vector at a position is a running sum, the residual stream, that each layer reads from and writes to. Consequences: the same vector space runs through the whole depth, so the final LayerNorm and unembedding can be applied after any layer and read as "what the model would have predicted here" (the logit lens); early exit is possible because the stream is already a valid state; and layers can be removed or skipped with graceful rather than catastrophic degradation. In an architecture without residuals none of that holds — each layer's output is a new space.

One position. Each block reads the stream, writes an increment back; the head at the top reads the sum. Reading after block 6 is the same operation applied early.
24Eight heads of 64 or one head of 512: which has more parameters?

The same. W_Q, W_K, W_V are d×d in both cases; multi-head just reshapes the projected d dimensions into h slices of d/h and runs the softmax separately in each slice. Heads cost no parameters; what they buy is h independent attention patterns per layer instead of one — eight places to look at once, at the same price.

25Why is a causal mask needed in training, when at generation time there is nothing to the right of the query anyway?

Because training does not generate. The whole target is given at once, and one forward pass produces T predictions from T positions — that is what makes transformer training parallel. Without the mask, position t would read position t+1, which is the answer it is supposed to predict; the loss would drop to zero and the model would learn to copy from one step ahead. The mask makes the parallel pass equivalent to T sequential ones. At generation the mask is still applied, and it is a no-op.

26In an SFT batch, is the loss computed on the prompt or only on the answer? How is that implemented?

It is a choice, and most instruction tuning computes it only on the answer: the prompt's positions in labels are set to −100, so the model is not trained to reproduce the instruction, only to continue it. Training on the prompt too is plain language modelling on the concatenation; it is not wrong, it just spends gradient on text the model will always be given. The collator that does this needs to know where the answer starts — a template boundary or the tokenised prompt length — which is the fragile part of every SFT pipeline.

27Packing: several documents concatenated into one 2 048-token sequence. What must the mask do for that to be honest — and what do pipelines do in practice?

Honest packing needs a block-diagonal causal mask: a token may see earlier tokens of its own document and nothing from the documents packed before it. Otherwise document 2 conditions on document 1, which is noise at best and, with EOS between them, teaches the model that unrelated text follows the end of a document. Many pipelines skip the block structure and accept the leakage — it costs little quality at scale. The correct version passes position_ids that restart at every document, which the FlashAttention variable-length kernels use to build the block mask without materialising it.

28What does temperature physically change? What happens at T → 0 and T → ∞?

It divides the logits before the softmax: softmax(z / T). Below 1 the gaps between logits widen and the distribution sharpens; at T → 0 it becomes the argmax, i.e. greedy decoding. Above 1 the gaps shrink; at T → ∞ every token is equally likely. It does not add knowledge or "creativity" — it reshapes the same ranking. Top-k and top-p are the other family: they truncate the tail instead of rescaling it, which is why they are usually combined with a temperature rather than replaced by one.

29Same text, two tokenizers, two perplexities. Can you compare them?

Not as they are. Perplexity is per token, and the two models tokenised the text into different numbers of tokens; a coarser tokenizer takes fewer, larger steps and gets more per-step surprise for the same total. Convert both to a unit that does not depend on the tokenizer: total negative log-likelihood of the text divided by its characters (bits per character) or by its words. Then they are comparable — they are both the probability the model assigns to the same string.

30Why does a transformer parallelise in training while an RNN does not — and why does that advantage disappear at generation?

An RNN's state at step t is a function of the state at t−1: the steps are sequential by definition. A transformer has no state between positions; with the whole sequence given, all positions are computed in one matrix multiply and the causal mask enforces the dependency order after the fact. At generation the sequence is not given — token t+1 cannot be computed until token t has been sampled — so the model runs one position per step, exactly like an RNN, and with a quadratically growing context to read.

31Why keep an encoder-decoder at all, if a decoder-only model reads the source through the same causal self-attention?

Three things differ. The encoder reads the source bidirectionally: every source token's representation sees the whole source; in a decoder-only model the source is read left to right, so the first source tokens are encoded without knowing what comes later. The encoder runs once, and its output is reused at every decoding step through cross-attention; a decoder-only model keeps the source in the same stream as the output. And the parameters are separated: the encoder can be trained and sized for reading, the decoder for writing. The cost is a second stack and a second attention type, which is why the decoder-only design won where the prefix is short relative to the pretraining corpus and simplicity matters.

32Greedy, beam search, sampling — when is beam search worse than sampling?

In open-ended generation. Beam search looks for the most probable sequence, and for text with many acceptable continuations the most probable one is generic, short and repetitive — the model assigns high probability to loops because each repeated token is individually likely. For tasks with one right answer — translation, summarisation, extractive QA — the search helps, because the target is narrow and beam finds it more often than greedy. The rule of thumb: beam when there is a correct output, sampling (with top-p) when there is a distribution of good ones.

33What is teacher forcing, and what does it cost at inference?

In training the decoder's input at every step is the gold previous token, not its own prediction — that is what allows the whole target in one pass. At inference the input is its own previous output, and the first mistake feeds the next step: the model is in a state it never saw in training, and errors compound along the sequence. That gap is called exposure bias. Scheduled sampling, sequence-level losses and RL fine-tuning are the attempts to close it; in practice large pretrained models are robust enough that most pipelines ignore it.

34Freeze the body and train only the head, or train everything: when does the frozen body win?

When the data is small, the task is close to what the pretraining already encodes, and the budget is tight. A frozen body cannot overfit the representation to a few hundred examples, trains in minutes, and can be shared by many heads. Full fine-tuning wins as soon as the task needs features the pretraining did not build — a new domain, token-level structure the body was not trained for — and there is enough data to move 110M parameters without memorising it. In between sits every partial option: top-k blocks, LoRA, adapters — each is a point on the same dial with a different memory bill.

35Why does a transformer need learning-rate warmup and logistic regression does not?

Two compounding reasons. Adam's second-moment estimate is built from a handful of steps at the start, so the early effective step sizes are large and noisy; and in a transformer a large step through the attention logits or through a Post-LN stack changes the scale of everything downstream — with Post-LN the model can diverge in the first hundred updates. Logistic regression is convex, has one layer and no softmax over scores to saturate; a big first step is just a big first step. Pre-LN architectures survive without warmup but still train better with a short one.

36Pre-LN and Post-LN: where does the LayerNorm sit in each, and why is almost every modern model Pre-LN?

Post-LN, the 2017 layout: x = LN(x + f(x)) — the norm is applied to the sum. Pre-LN: x = x + f(LN(x)) — the norm is applied to the input of the sublayer, and the residual path is left untouched. With Pre-LN the residual stream is an unnormalised sum all the way up, gradients reach the bottom layers through the identity path, and deep stacks train without a carefully tuned warmup. Post-LN can end slightly better when it does train, which is why it survived in BERT, but it is fragile at depth. The one thing Pre-LN adds is a final LayerNorm before the head, since the stream itself is never normalised.

37Dropout during fine-tuning — on or off? And during generation?

On during fine-tuning (the pretrained config keeps it, typically 0.1), off during evaluation and generation. The switch is model.eval(), and it changes more than gradients: dropout stops sampling masks, and the same input gives the same output. Forgetting it is the classic bug behind "the model gives different answers to the same prompt at temperature 0" and "validation numbers are lower than expected". Trainer and generate call it for you; a hand-written loop must.

38Gradient accumulation: batch 8 with 4 accumulation steps equals batch 32? Where does the equality break?

Almost. The gradients add, so the update is the same — provided each micro-batch loss is scaled by ¼ (or the accumulated gradient divided by 4). It breaks where the loss depends on batch composition: BatchNorm statistics (transformers have none), dynamic padding (each micro-batch pads to its own longest), and — the one that bit real pipelines — token-weighted losses: a mean over tokens per micro-batch, then averaged, is not the mean over all tokens when the micro-batches hold different token counts. Long sequences get under-weighted. The fix is to normalise by the total token count of the whole accumulated batch.

39Why does a model that never saw an end-of-sequence token in training never stop generating?

Because stopping is a prediction like any other: the model emits EOS when EOS is the most likely (or sampled) next token, and a token that never appeared in the training targets has a learned logit that is arbitrarily low. The generation loop then runs to max_new_tokens and the output trails into whatever the model thinks follows. It usually comes from stripping EOS in preprocessing or from the collator masking it as a pad — see the pad-equals-EOS question.

40Zero-shot classification with a decoder: the label Sci/Tech is three tokens, World is one. What decides the argmax?

Tokenisation, not the text. The score of a label is the sum of its tokens' log-probabilities, and a sum of three negative numbers is smaller than a sum of one almost regardless of content — the multi-token label loses by construction. Two cheap fixes: divide by the token count (a per-token score), or pick label words that are one token each. The second is what a verbalizer is: the mapping from a class to the word the model will actually be scored on, chosen so that the words are comparable.

41What are token_type_ids, and why does GPT-2 not have them?

A third embedding in BERT, added to the token and position embeddings: 0 for segment A, 1 for segment B. It exists because next-sentence prediction needed the model to know where the boundary is, and a learned segment embedding was the simplest way to mark it. A decoder-only model marks boundaries in the text itself — a separator, a template, a newline — because the prefix is the only interface it has; there is nothing that a separate embedding could add that a token cannot. Most encoders after BERT (RoBERTa) dropped it as well, together with NSP.

42Extractive QA with a span head versus a generated answer: what does the head guarantee that the generator does not?

That the answer is a substring of the paragraph. The head scores two positions and the prediction is a span between them; it cannot say a word that is not there. A generator usually copies and nothing forces it to — it can paraphrase, complete, or invent. Whether that is a feature or a failure depends on the product: a guaranteed substring is what you want when the answer is evidence, a free answer is what you want when the answer is a synthesis. What to measure on the generator side is the share of answers that are not substrings — fidelity, before exact match.

43Entities that nest — Bank of America, where America is also a location. Encoder or decoder, and why?

A BIO head cannot express it: one tag per word, and America is either inside the organisation or a location, not both. Ways out on the encoder side are a span classifier over all O(L²) spans, or a separate head per type — both are more machinery than one tag per token. A generator writes the structure directly: [ORG Bank of [LOC America]], nesting for free. That is the pattern to look for when choosing: if the output space has structure that a label per position cannot carry, the task has moved to the prefix.

44Your fine-tuned classifier reports 0.95 on validation. What is the first question to ask before believing it?

What the baseline gets. A TF-IDF + logistic regression on the same split is minutes of work and on news-topic classification lands at 0.92; if the transformer is at 0.95, the transformer bought three points, not ninety-five. Then the two hygiene checks: is the class balance such that the majority class alone scores high, and are there near-duplicates between train and validation (news wires, product reviews and scraped corpora all have them). Only after those is 0.95 a number about the model.

45One formula for three weeks. In seq2seq cross-attention, in BERT, and in GPT — what is the query, and what are the keys?

Cross-attention: the query is a decoder position (the target so far), the keys and values are the encoder's outputs — every source position. BERT: the query is a token, the keys are all tokens of the input, both sides, itself included. GPT: the query is a token, the keys are the tokens on its left, itself included — the prompt as well as the text it has generated. Same softmax-weighted sum of values; the three models differ only in which set the keys come from, and that set is what the mask encodes.

46Lightning round. Encoder or decoder, and what you give up: (a) sentiment of a million reviews a day, latency matters; (b) spelling correction where words may split or merge; (c) NER with a new entity type every month; (d) an answer that must be a substring; (e) classes that change every week; (f) reranking documents for search.

(a) Encoder with a classification head: one pass per review, calibrated probabilities. You give up nothing the task needs. (b) Decoder: a label per input word cannot say "these two are one" — the encoder is at zero on split and merge by construction. You give up one pass per word and the guarantee that correct words stay untouched. (c) Decoder with the types in the prompt, or an encoder retrained monthly — the head is a matrix with a fixed number of rows. (d) Encoder span head: the substring guarantee is the point. (e) Decoder or zero-shot through the vocabulary: no head to retrain. (f) Encoder cross-encoder (query and document in one input, one score): a pass per pair, but the pass is cheap and the score is a number, not a token.

The correct letter matters less than the named price. "Decoder, because LLM" with no cost attached is the answer to watch for.
