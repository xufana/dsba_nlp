Block 01 · what we want from a vector

What we want from a vector

Before any method, the demand. A classifier can do only a few things with its input: add it up, compare it, draw a line through it. So whatever we turn a string into has to be an object those operations make sense on — numeric, of a fixed size, and with a geometry that means something. The last one is the whole difficulty. We want like to sit near love and both of them far from geografia; we want the vector of a text to be buildable from the vectors of its words; we want it cheap, at a million documents; and we want it from the data we actually have, which is text, mostly without labels.

The first thing anyone tries is to number the words. That pretends cat = 7 is closer to dog = 8 than to house = 200, so the next move is to give every word its own axis instead: one-hot, a vector of |V| zeros and a single 1. It is numeric and fixed-size, and it is useless as a representation of meaning. Every two words are at exactly the same distance, so like is as far from love as from geografia. Whatever the model learns about love tells it nothing about like. And a word that was not in the vocabulary has no axis at all — not a bad vector, no vector; two different unknown words are the same nothing. The dimension is the size of the vocabulary, tens of thousands, almost all zeros.

One-hot · three words|V| axes, one per word
Three words, three axes. The dot product of any two rows is 0: the representation knows that the words differ, and nothing else.

There are two objects to represent, and they want different things. A word vector should carry meaning — the geometry above, which we cannot read off the word itself and have to get from somewhere: from its contexts in a corpus, or from the labels of a task. A text vector should make the operation cheap — classification, search, deduplication — and it has to turn a variable number of words into one fixed-size object: a sum, an average, or something learned. That second step is a decision of its own, and it is where a good part of the meaning can get lost. We'll cover it later, stay tuned.

Every method of this week makes four combinations: how the numbers are made — counted from the text, or learned by a model — and what gets a vector, a word or a text.

First, counted, per text: the bag of words and tf-idf, no semantics, very cheap, and the strongest baseline for the text-classification and regression tasks (speaking of the cost-quality tradeoff). Next, learned, per word: word2vec, GloVe, fastText — meaning by substitutability, learned from contexts. Though they are learned per word, next they can be assembled per text: mean pooling, the cheapest bridge between the two objects. Learned per occurrence: the contextual models of week 4, where bank finally gets two vectors. Each cell bought something and paid for something; none of them replaced the others.

How do we get from word vectors to one vector for a text? The simple ways are all arithmetic over the word vectors: sum, mean, max per coordinate, a weighted mean (by idf, so rare words count more), concatenation, or keeping one word (the last, the first). The requirements come from what the classifier needs: one fixed size for every text; nothing new to train, so it works the moment you have word vectors; every word contributing; no dependence on length, or a long review and a short one with the same content end up in different places; and similar texts close together. Check them yourself — every number below is computed in your browser on toy 2-d word vectors:

Concatenation fails the first requirement at once, the last word ignores every other word and depends on order, the sum fails length, max keeps only the loudest word per coordinate. Mean and weighted mean pass all five — that is why mean pooling is the default bridge. And the last column fails for every row: no arithmetic over word vectors knows that not flips good, because a sum does not know which vector stood next to which. Order and composition need a model that reads the sequence — that is where weeks 2–4 go.

Block 02 · the vector

What is the vector, actually?

A bag of words represents a document as one number per vocabulary word. The vocabulary is the columns; each document is a row. That is the whole idea — the document becomes a point in a space with one axis per word. Two things are already gone and will not come back: word order and anything not in the vocabulary.

Raw counts are a poor number to put in a cell, for two reasons. A long document has bigger counts for everything, so it looks different from a short one about the same thing. And a word that occurs in every document — the, today — gets large counts while telling us nothing about which document this is. tf-idf fixes both, in three steps.

Term frequency divides the count by the length of the document: tf(t,d)=N(t,d)|d|, how much of this document the word is.

Inverse document frequency measures how much the word distinguishes documents across the corpus: idf(t)=ln|D|df(t), where df(t) is the number of documents that contain t. A word in one document of four gets ln,4; a word in all four gets ln,1=0.

Multiply, then normalise: the cell is tf·idf, and each row is divided by its length, so that every document is a vector of length 1. Libraries usually smooth the idf so that it never reaches exactly zero; the idea does not change. Click a cell below to see what is written in it and where it came from.

Four documents · tf-idf
Columns are the vocabulary, sorted; rows are the documents. A cell is tf × idf, divided by the length of its row. Computed in your browser.

Where the columns come from matters as much as what goes in them. The vocabulary is collected once, from the training documents, and fixed: every later document is mapped onto those columns. Before anything is counted the text goes through preprocessing — usually lowercasing and splitting into tokens — and whatever the preprocessing merges or drops never reaches the vector.

The last step deserves a second look. Normalising each row to length 1 means the vector says in what proportions the document uses its words, and nothing about how long it is.

Block 03 · normalisation

Stemming, lemmatization and the rest of preprocessing

A bag of words gives every surface form its own column. study, studies, studied, studying are four columns, and what the model learns about one of them says nothing about the others — the one-hot problem again, one level up. In English it is a nuisance. In Russian, where a noun has a dozen forms, it splits the statistics of every word a dozen ways, and most forms are seen a handful of times. Normalisation maps the forms of a word onto one key before anything is counted.

There are two ways to do it. Stemming cuts endings off by rules, with no dictionary: Porter's algorithm (1980) is a cascade of suffix rules. It is fast and works on any string, including words nobody has seen — but the stem is not necessarily a word, and the rules know nothing about meaning. Lemmatization maps a word to its dictionary form, the lemma, using a dictionary and the word's part of speech: went → go, better → good. It needs a morphological analyser for each language, and without the part of speech it has to guess.

One word, three normalisationsstem · lemma · lemma without POS
Porter's stemmer and the WordNet lemmatizer, run on these words. The last column is the same lemmatizer without the part of speech. For Russian, the Snowball stemmer.

Read the table row by row. The stemmer merges university and universe into univers, and organization into organ — two different words in one column. It cannot touch went or better, whose forms share no ending to cut; a lemmatizer can, because it looks the word up. Without the part of speech the lemmatizer falls back to treating every word as a noun and leaves went alone, and was loses its s like a plural. In Russian the stemmer does well on the regular forms of кот and gives three different stems to шёл, иду, пошла — three forms of one verb, идти, that only a dictionary knows belong together.

Normalisation is a bet that what it merges is not what the task needs. It shrinks the vocabulary and pools the statistics of rare forms, which pays off when there is little labelled data to learn each form on its own, and in search, where a query and a document have to share a form to match at all — treating diabetes never meets treatment of diabetic without it. It loses whatever the form carried — tense, number, and sometimes the meaning, when two words collapse into one stem. With plenty of data the model learns each form on its own and the gain disappears. Preprocessing is chosen by the representation and the task, not by habit.

Stemming is one step of a longer list that usually goes under "clean your text": lowercasing, removing stopwords (the most frequent function words — the, and, of, and, in most lists, not), stripping punctuation and numbers, removing markup. The usual advice is to do all of them, and the usual advice is wrong roughly half the time. Every step deletes something. For one task that something is noise, for another it is the signal: casing, function words and punctuation are noise for a topic and the very thing that tells two authors apart or a company from a fruit. And a text is not always long enough to spare it — in a long review one deleted word changes little, in a one-line message it can be most of the evidence there is.

Block 04 · the long tail

Zipf's law, unknown words, and the distributional hypothesis

Count every word in the 120 000 headlines of AG News — {zipf_tokens} word occurrences — and sort the {zipf_types} distinct words by frequency. A handful of words are everywhere: {zipf_top}. The 100 most frequent words make up {zipf_top100} of all the text. At the other end the list never ends: {zipf_hapax_pct} of the distinct words occur exactly once.

This shape has a name. Zipf's law: the frequency of the r-th most frequent word is roughly proportional to 1/r — the second word is half as frequent as the first, the tenth a tenth as frequent. On log-log axes that is a straight line with slope −1; on these headlines the slope is {zipf_slope}. It holds for every language and almost every corpus anyone has counted.

Rank × frequency · AG News, training setlog-log
Every distinct word of the AG News training set, by rank and count, both axes logarithmic. The dashed line has slope −1. (39 among the top words is the debris of &#39;, an HTML apostrophe.)

For a representation built by counting, Zipf's law is the bill. The head of the curve is a few dozen function words: they dominate every count and say almost nothing about the topic, which is why idf downweights them and stopword lists exist. The tail is most of the vocabulary: words seen once or twice, each a column with almost no examples to learn its weight from. And the tail does not end when the data grows — a bigger corpus moves the curve to the right and brings new words with it, so there is always a word the training set never saw.

A word the training set never contained. It happens three different ways — a misspelling, a name nobody had used yet, a rare form of a word everybody knows — and the question is not whether it happens. On clean English news with a large training set it is a fraction of a percent of test tokens (on AG News, {oov_rate_pct}), mostly timestamps and article codes; on Russian, on chat text, on a small training set it is an order of magnitude more. The question is what a representation does when it happens.

For a bag of words nothing raises. The new word has no column, so the row is computed as if the word were not there. The model is not wrong about the word, it is blind to it — and blindness is silent, which is the dangerous part: a product name, a new disease, a typo in the one word that carried the label all vanish without a trace in the logs. word2vec at least fails loudly: it has no row for the word and raises an error.

There are three families of answers. Normalise the word to something that was seen: stemming and lemmatisation map the forms of a word onto one stem or lemma, so walked meets walking. Change the unit: cut words into pieces that are always known — character n-grams, or the subwords of BPE, which falls back to single characters if it must, so nothing is ever out of vocabulary. Build the vector from pieces: fastText represents a word as the sum of the vectors of its character n-grams, so an unseen word still gets a vector from the pieces it shares with seen ones. And word2vec has none of these: one row per training word, and nothing for anything else.

Read the stemming row first, because it is the one the textbook gets wrong. Porter turns recieved into reciev, and no training word stems to that — the stem of received is receiv. Stemming rescues morphology, not spelling: unbelievability → unbeliev, which is also the stem of unbelievable, a training word, so that one is saved. A misspelling and a new name are not. Character n-grams never fail completely — {ng_recieved} of the {ng_total_recieved} n-grams of recieved were seen in training, as pieces of received, believed, achieved. BPE never fails at all: {bpe_recieved}, pieces that are in the vocabulary by construction. fastText builds the vector as the sum of {ft_recieved} n-gram vectors, and recieved lands next to received because they share most of them.

Every fix has a bill, and the bill is the same in different currencies: things that look alike are treated alike, whether or not they are alike. Character n-grams pay in dimension — a news corpus has tens of thousands of words and hundreds of thousands of distinct 3–5-grams, most of which separate nothing. fastText pays in the vector space: recieved is near received, good, but so is any pair that shares a long substring. Stemming pays in collisions, universe and university in one column, and buys nothing for spelling. BPE moves the cost from the vocabulary into the sequence: nothing is ever unknown, but a rare word becomes several tokens, each a step to compute and a place to be wrong, and the pieces have no meaning of their own — rec is in record and recipe as well.

Zipf's law says that learning each word from its own occurrences runs out of data for most of the vocabulary. The way round is to let words share what is learned about them — and for that we need to know which words are alike, without anyone labelling them.

The answer is the distributional hypothesis: "you shall know a word by the company it keeps" (Firth 1957; Harris 1954 for the formal version). Words that occur in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. The meaning of a word is not observable; its contexts are, in every sentence ever written. A rare word appears a few times, but each time among frequent, well-known words, and those contexts tell us which known words it resembles. Every method in the next block is this hypothesis turned into a loss.

Block 05 · the embedding

Word2vec: skip-gram, CBOW, and fastText

One-hot gave every word its own axis and no similarity at all. Word2vec (Mikolov et al. 2013) gives every word a short dense vector — a hundred or three hundred numbers — and learns those numbers from raw text, so that words used alike end up close. It is the distributional hypothesis turned into a prediction task, in two mirror-image versions.

Skip-gram takes one word — the centre — and predicts the words around it. A window, say two words on each side, slides over the text, and every (centre, context) pair inside it is one training example: oil prices rise today with the centre prices gives (prices, oil), (prices, rise), (prices, today).

The model is two matrices. Win, of size |V| × d, holds one row per word. The centre word comes in as a one-hot vector, and multiplying it by Win just picks out its row — that row, vc, is the word's current vector, and it is called h while it passes through the network. Wout, of size d × |V|, holds one column uw per word. Multiplying h by Wout gives a score uw·vc for every word of the vocabulary, and a softmax turns the scores into a distribution over possible context words:

P(o|c)=exp(uo·vc)∑wexp(uw·vc)

We're gonna work in pairs: one word that comes in, one word that comes out. For skip-gram there going to be the same centre word and n different outputs as a second word in the pair. So, the model will see one input and learn to give n different outputs. The loss of one pair is the cross-entropy against the context word that was actually there, −logP(o|c): a |V|-way classification, the same softmax as any classifier, with the neighbouring word as the label.

Skip-gramthe centre predicts each context word
One training pair: centre oil, context prices. The highlighted row of Win is the vector of oil; the highlighted column of Wout belongs to prices.

Training is plain gradient descent on that loss, one pair at a time. Both matrices start as small random numbers — nothing about language is put in. For a pair (c, o), the gradient moves the centre's row vc towards uo and away from the columns of the other words in proportion to how much probability they took, and moves uo towards vc. Two words that keep appearing next to the same context words receive the same pushes and drift together: the distributional hypothesis, executed by the optimiser. The derivation of these gradients, step by step, is in Stanford CS224N, Lecture 2: Word Vector Representations — word2vec.

The prediction task itself is a pretext. Nobody needs a model that guesses the neighbours of a word, and it never guesses them well. What we want is the by-product: the rows of Win.

CBOW — continuous bag of words — is the mirror image: the context predicts the centre. The rows of all context words are taken from Win and averaged into one vector h; their order is lost on the way, hence "bag of words". h goes through Wout and a softmax over the vocabulary, now scored against the centre word. One window is one training example, where skip-gram made one example per context word.

The difference shows up on frequency. CBOW is faster and smooths over frequent words. Skip-gram gives every occurrence of a rare word its own updates, one per neighbour, and represents rare words better (Mikolov et al. 2013): for CBOW there going to be only one run with one central word, while for Skip-Gram we'll see it n times.

CBOWthe averaged context predicts the centre
One window: the context oil, prices, sharply, today predicts the centre rise. Four rows of the same Win are averaged into h.

One practical problem remains. The softmax over the whole vocabulary costs one dot product per vocabulary word — tens of thousands — for every single training pair. The same paper replaced it with a cheaper task, negative sampling: for each real pair (c, o), draw k random words (five to twenty, sampled by frequency) and train a yes/no classifier — the sigmoid of the dot product — to say yes to the real pair and no to the random ones:

logσ(uo·vc)+∑klogσ(−uk·vc)

The two matrices stay; only the loss changes, from one |V|-way softmax to k + 1 sigmoids.

Word2vec has one row per word of the training vocabulary and nothing for anything else, and it treats walk, walks, walked as three unrelated rows. fastText (Bojanowski et al. 2017) keeps the skip-gram objective and changes what the vector of a word is: the sum of the vectors of its character n-grams. The word is wrapped in boundary marks and cut into pieces — for where with n = 3: <wh, whe, her, ere, re>, plus the whole word <where>; by default n runs from 3 to 6. Every piece has its own learned vector, and the word's vector is their sum.

Two things follow. Pieces are shared between words, so walked and walking share most of their parameters and rare forms borrow from frequent ones. And a word never seen in training still gets a vector — the sum of the pieces it shares with seen words. To keep memory bounded, n-grams are hashed into a fixed number of buckets, two million by default; two different n-grams can land in one bucket, and that collision is accepted.

fastTexta word is the sum of its character n-grams
The vector of where is the sum of six vectors from one table of n-gram vectors. That table — not a table of words — is what fastText learns.

When training ends, the task is thrown away and Win is kept: a table with one vector per word of the vocabulary — or, for fastText, a table of n-gram vectors that yields a vector for any string — learned from raw text with no labels at all.
