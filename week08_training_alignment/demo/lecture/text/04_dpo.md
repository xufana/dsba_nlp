# dpo · DPO and the "RL without RL" family

## dpo-p1

**The idea.** The RLHF objective with a KL penalty has an analytically optimal policy: the reference re-weighted by the exponentiated reward. Turn that around, and the reward can be written through the policy — and then the reward model and PPO are not needed at all (Rafailov et al. 2023). ## dpo-p2

In essence a classification loss: raise the probability of the good answer relative to the bad one, **counting from the reference**. No reward model, no sampling, no critic.

**What it is for.** Preference tuning without the RLHF infrastructure: open-source models, domain fine-tunes, style, refusals. It is as stable as an ordinary fine-tune.

## q-dpo-models · sort

People say "DPO needs two models". Which of PPO's four does DPO still keep?

bins: PPO and DPO · PPO only

- the policy being trained → PPO and DPO
- the frozen reference → PPO and DPO
- the reward model → PPO only
- the value model (critic) → PPO only

> Two of the four, because two models appear in the loss. $π_θ$ is the policy, and the gradients go through it. $π_{ref}$ is a frozen copy of the SFT model — the point everything is counted from, and it plays the role that the KL penalty played in RLHF: the probability of the good answer is raised *relative to how the original model rated it*. The reward model is gone because the reward is implicit — $β\,\mathrm{log}\,π_θ/π_{ref}$ **is** the reward. The critic is gone because there is no RL: no sampling, nothing to credit token by token.
> miss: if the reference went to "PPO only": it is easy to think DPO dropped the KL penalty along with PPO. It moved it inside the loss — that is what the ratios are.

## q-dpo-noref · pick

Drop the reference from the loss — use $\mathrm{log}\,π_θ(y_w|x) − \mathrm{log}\,π_θ(y_l|x)$ alone. What happens in training?

- [ ] nothing important: the reference was a constant anyway
- [x] the loss can be driven down by pushing the rejected answers' probability towards zero, wherever that takes the model
- [ ] training becomes on-policy
- [ ] it turns into SFT on the chosen answers

> Without the reference the loss is minimised by the model leaving its starting distribution in any direction that widens the gap. Pushing a rejected answer's probability towards zero is the cheapest way to do it, and nothing in the loss says what happens to everything else the model writes. With the reference, every change is measured against the SFT model's own opinion of the same answer, so a pair the SFT model already got right asks for little, and a big move from the start costs.
> miss: if "a constant": it is a constant per pair, but a different one for the chosen and the rejected answer — and it is exactly what makes the loss about *change* rather than about absolute probability.

## dpo-p3

**Two caveats about the two models.**

- **In practice it can be one model.** The reference is frozen and the dataset is fixed, so $\mathrm{log}\,π_{ref}(y_w|x)$ and $\mathrm{log}\,π_{ref}(y_l|x)$ can be computed once, before training (TRL's `precompute_ref_log_probs`). With LoRA the reference is the same model with the adapter switched off.
- **ORPO and SimPO remove the reference altogether.** ORPO adds an SFT loss and does SFT and preferences in one step; SimPO normalises the log-probability by the length of the answer and adds a margin. The price is a weaker anchor and a higher risk of degradation.

## q-dpo-family · sort

Which variant fits each situation?

bins: IPO · KTO · ORPO · SimPO · online DPO

- you only have thumbs up / thumbs down from your product, never two answers to the same prompt → KTO
- after DPO the model's answers get longer with every epoch → SimPO
- memory is tight and you want one training stage instead of SFT followed by DPO → ORPO
- the pairs were written by another model a year ago, and your model's mistakes are not in them → online DPO
- almost every pair has a clear winner, and DPO overfits them → IPO

> One property each. KTO drops the need for pairs; SimPO divides by length, so a long answer does not win by being long; ORPO folds the SFT loss in and drops the reference; online DPO regenerates the pairs from the current model, so they show its own mistakes; IPO changes the loss so that a pair the model already separates well does not keep pushing the margin up. In practice several of these are combined — and the plain DPO baseline is often hard to beat; measure before you switch.
> miss: if online DPO went to the long answers: regenerating the pairs does not remove the judge's preference for length — the new pairs will prefer long answers just as much.

## dpo-p4

**The problems of DPO.**

- **Offline.** The pairs are fixed, often written by another model: no exploration, and a distribution shift between the answers in the data and the answers the model writes (the block on on- and off-policy is about this).
- Often the probability of **both** the chosen and the rejected answer falls — the rejected one faster. The loss only sees the difference, and the model can drift.
- Overfitting, sensitivity to $β$.
- Reward hacking is implicit: the length bias and sycophancy in the data go straight into the model.
- On reasoning and code it usually loses to well-tuned online RL.

## q-both-down · pick

During DPO the log-probability of the chosen answers and of the rejected answers both go down, the rejected ones faster. The loss is going down. Is something broken?

- [ ] yes — the chosen answers must go up, or the loss is wrong
- [x] not necessarily — the loss only sees the difference, and probability can leave both answers for answers that are in neither half of any pair
- [ ] no — falling probabilities mean the model is becoming more confident

> This is a known property of DPO, not a bug of one run (Razin et al. 2024 call it *likelihood displacement*). The gap is what the loss rewards; where the probability goes is not in the loss. It usually goes to other answers — which can be fine, or can be the drift that makes a DPO model worse at things nobody paired. That is why people watch $\mathrm{log}\,π(y_w)$ during training, and why several variants add a term that stops it from falling.
> miss: if "more confident": confidence would be probability *concentrating* on some answer. Here it is leaving both answers you can see; you cannot tell from the pair where it went.
