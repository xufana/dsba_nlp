# policy · On-policy and off-policy

## pol-p1

Every method so far has a second property besides its signal, and it explains a good part of how they behave: **whose answers it learns from**.

The **policy** $π_θ$ is the model itself; an action is the next token, a trajectory is an answer $y$.

- **On-policy**: we learn from the answers of the **current** version of the model. Generate → score → update → throw the old data away.
- **Off-policy**: we learn from the answers of **another** policy — an older version, a different model, people.
- **Offline** is the extreme case: the dataset is fixed, there are no new generations at all.

An analogy. On-policy is going over **your own** chess games. Off-policy is going over a grandmaster's games: there are many of them and they are cheap, but you never see your own typical mistakes.

## pol-p2

**Why it matters mathematically.** We optimise an expectation over **our own** distribution. If the data come from another policy $μ$, we need **importance sampling**: every sample is re-weighted by how much more likely our policy is to write it than the one that did.

## pol-p3

For a long answer this ratio is a product of per-token ratios. Even when the two policies differ a little at each token, the product over hundreds of tokens explodes or collapses — and the estimate is carried by a handful of samples with enormous weights. ## pol-p4

That is why off-policy RL is unstable, and why every RL method for LLMs stays close to on-policy. PPO and GRPO take a few gradient steps on each freshly generated batch, so by the second step the data are already slightly off-policy; they use the per-token ratio $π_θ/π_{old}$ and **clip** it, so that the update cannot move far from the policy that generated the data. SFT and DPO on a fixed dataset do not re-weight at all — they simply train on someone else's answers, and pay for it in distribution shift.

## q-pol-sort · sort

Where does each method sit?

bins: on-policy, or nearly · in between · off-policy / offline

- SFT on human demonstrations → off-policy / offline
- PPO → on-policy, or nearly
- GRPO → on-policy, or nearly
- DPO on a fixed set of pairs → off-policy / offline
- online / iterative DPO, pairs regenerated by the current model → in between | on-policy, or nearly
- rejection sampling: the model writes many answers, the good ones become SFT data → in between

> SFT and DPO train on answers written before training; exposure bias and distribution shift are the bill. PPO and GRPO sample from the current model every step — "nearly", because a batch is reused for a few steps, which is exactly what the clipping is for. Online DPO regenerates the pairs every so often, so it moves towards on-policy as often as you regenerate. Rejection sampling is the honest hybrid: the data are the model's own, but the training is SFT on the selected answers.
> miss: if DPO on pairs sampled from your SFT model went to "on-policy": the pairs were sampled once, before the first step; after a few hundred updates the model they came from is gone. On-policy is about who writes the answers *at the step*.

## q-ppo-clip · pick

Why does PPO clip the ratio $π_θ/π_{old}$?

- [x] so that a few updates on one batch cannot move the policy far from the one that generated it — where the importance correction stops being reliable
- [ ] to keep the policy close to the SFT model
- [ ] to normalise the rewards across prompts

> The clip is the off-policy guard. The batch was written by $π_{old}$; the moment we take a gradient step, we are learning from slightly someone else's data, and the ratio corrects for that — reliably only while it stays near 1. Clipping stops the update from earning anything by pushing it further. Keeping the policy near the SFT model is the KL term's job, a different leash to a different model; normalising the rewards is the advantage's job.
> miss: if "close to the SFT model": the two leashes are easy to merge. $π_{old}$ is the policy from a few steps ago and changes all the time; $π_{ref}$ is the SFT model and never changes.

And the connection to the previous block: the on-policy nature is why RL usually generalises better than SFT and offline DPO — the model gets a signal about **its own** mistakes in **its own** distribution.

## q-gpt4-pairs · pick

You have 100 000 preference pairs written by a strong model last year, and you run DPO on your 1B model. The gains are small. What is the most likely reason?

- [x] the pairs show the strong model's choices and mistakes, not the ones your model makes — there is little signal about its own behaviour
- [ ] 100 000 pairs are too few for DPO
- [ ] DPO cannot train a model this small

> Distribution shift. The rejected answers in the data are a strong model's near-misses; your 1B model's typical failures look nothing like them, so the loss spends itself on pushing down answers your model would never write. The fix is the on-policy move: sample the pairs from your own model and have them judged — that is what online and iterative DPO do, and why Tülu 3's preference mixture includes answers sampled from its own SFT model.
> miss: if "too few": the size is fine — many published DPO runs use fewer. It is *whose* answers they are.
