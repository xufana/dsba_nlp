From next token to assistant,
as questions

A pretrained model continues text; the model you talk to answers, refuses and admits it does not know. This page is about what happens in between: which signal each stage of post-training uses, what it is good for, and where each of them breaks.

The same deal as on the other lecture pages: every block first explains, then asks. Commit to an answer before you open it — if the answer is not the one you expected, that is the place to reread or to ask us. Your answers stay in this browser, so you can leave and come back.

Block 00 · the goal

What alignment is

After pretraining the model can continue text — the way text continues on the internet. It knows a lot, but:

  • it does not necessarily answer a question: it may continue it with ten more questions, like a forum thread;
  • it writes the useful, the toxic and the made-up with the same readiness;
  • it does not know which answer we consider good.

Alignment is the training after pretraining that moves the distribution of answers from "how it happens on the internet" to "how we want it". The usual target is HHH: helpful, honest, harmless.

An analogy. Pretraining is a person who has read the whole library. Alignment is their first job, where they are told how to talk to clients, what is allowed and what is not, and that an honest "I don't know" is better than a confident invention. Almost no new knowledge is added — the behaviour changes.

It happens in stages, and the stages differ in one thing: what signal says which answer is good. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) shows the answer — "write this". Preference optimisation compares two answers — "this one is better than that one". RL on verifiable tasks runs a check — "right" or "wrong". Safety data, Constitutional AI and red teaming are fed into the preference stage.

Post-training, stage by stagewhat says which answer is good
Each stage gets less data than the one before it, and a narrower signal: trillions of tokens of text, then tens or hundreds of thousands of demonstrations, comparisons and checked answers.

The practical consequence is the one people miss most often: if the model lacks a fact, post-training is the wrong tool. A fact the model needs goes into the prompt, or into a document retrieved for the prompt. Teaching it through fine-tuning mostly teaches a style of answering — we will see in the next block what that does to hallucinations.

Block 01 · "write this"

Supervised fine-tuning

The idea. Pairs "instruction → reference answer", and the usual next-token loss — computed only on the tokens of the answer. The model reads the whole dialogue through a chat template: special markers say where the system prompt, the user's turn and the assistant's turn begin and end. All of it is context, and only the assistant's answer is a target: every other position gets the label that the loss ignores. The model is not asked to predict what the user will write; it is asked to predict what the assistant writes, given everything before it.

What it is for. The dialogue format, following instructions, style, basic refusals. It is the cheap way to give the model a "character" — LIMA (Zhou et al. 2023) got a usable assistant out of 1 000 carefully chosen examples.

The problems. Each comes from the same fact: SFT can only say "write this".

  • Good demonstrations are expensive: writing a reference answer is harder than choosing the better of two.
  • It only teaches how to do it, never how not to: there is no negative signal.
  • It imitates the surface. If the data contains facts the model did not know after pretraining, it is taught to answer confidently without anything to back the answer — and that provokes hallucinations (Gekhman et al. 2024).
  • The ceiling is the quality of the annotators.
  • Exposure bias: the model trains on perfect prefixes — the reference answer up to position t — and generates on its own. After its first mistake it is continuing a prefix unlike any in the training data.

Block 02 · "this one is better"

RLHF, RLAIF and Constitutional AI

Most of what we want from an assistant — helpful but not wordy, confident but not invented, the balance between helping and refusing — is hard to demonstrate and easy to compare. So the next stage learns from comparisons.

Step 1 — a reward model. People see two answers to the same prompt and pick the better one. The reward model r(x,y) — usually the SFT model with its vocabulary head replaced by one number — is trained on the picks with the Bradley–Terry model: every answer has a hidden score, and the probability that yw wins over yl depends only on the difference of the scores. In effect, logistic regression on a pair.

The reward model · Bradley & Terry (1952) P(yw≻yl∣x)=σ(r(x,yw)−r(x,yl))

Step 2 — RL with a KL penalty to the SFT model; the optimiser is PPO. The model generates answers, the reward model scores them, and the policy is pushed towards answers with high reward — while the KL term keeps it close to the SFT model it started from, the reference. Without the leash the policy finds the blind spots of the reward model: the reward model is just a network trained on a few hundred thousand comparisons, and outside them its scores mean nothing.

What it is for. The subtle properties: tone, helpfulness, when to help and when to refuse. Unlike SFT the training is on-policy — the model learns from its own answers — and there is a negative signal: a low reward pushes an answer down.

RL with a KL penalty · Ouyang et al. (2022) maxπ𝔼y∼π[r(x,y)]−βKL(π∥πref)
What PPO keeps in memoryfour models of the policy's size
Four networks of the size of the policy, and generation inside the training loop. The value model (critic) estimates how good a half-written answer is, so that each token can be credited with part of the final reward.

The problems.

  • Four models in memory: policy, reference, reward and value. PPO is unstable and sensitive to hyperparameters.
  • Reward hacking, Goodhart's law: once a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure. The policy finds what the reward model likes regardless of quality — long answers, extra formatting, and sycophancy: agreeing with the user.
  • The annotators are noisy and disagree with each other; they pick the answer that sounds convincing, not the one that is correct.
  • Choosing β: a weak KL means degradation, a strong one means the model does not learn.
  • Alignment tax: some capabilities sometimes drop.

RLAIF and Constitutional AI. The idea: replace the human, partly, with a model. In Constitutional AI (Bai et al. 2022) the model gets a set of written principles:

  1. SL phase. The model critiques and rewrites its own answers according to the principles, and is fine-tuned on the rewritten ones.
  2. RL phase. A judge model compares pairs of answers according to the principles; its picks train the reward model; then RL as above.

What it is for. Scaling the labelling, especially for safety. The principles are explicit and can be edited — the rules of the model are a document, not a crowd's implicit taste.

The problems. The judge has biases of its own: it prefers its own style (self-preference) and it likes length. Principles conflict and are hard to formulate. And there is a closed loop: the model learns to please a model.

Block 03 · RL without RL

DPO and its family

The idea. The RLHF objective with a KL penalty has an analytically optimal policy: the reference re-weighted by the exponentiated reward. Turn that around, and the reward can be written through the policy — and then the reward model and PPO are not needed at all (Rafailov et al. 2023). Substitute that reward into Bradley–Terry, and what is left is a loss on pairs:

Rafailov et al. (2023) ℒDPO=−logσ(βlogπθ(yw∣x)πref(yw∣x)−βlogπθ(yl∣x)πref(yl∣x))

In essence a classification loss: raise the probability of the good answer relative to the bad one, counting from the reference. No reward model, no sampling, no critic.

What it is for. Preference tuning without the RLHF infrastructure: open-source models, domain fine-tunes, style, refusals. It is as stable as an ordinary fine-tune.

Two caveats about the two models.

  • In practice it can be one model. The reference is frozen and the dataset is fixed, so log,πref(yw|x) and log,πref(yl|x) can be computed once, before training (TRL's precompute_ref_log_probs). With LoRA the reference is the same model with the adapter switched off.
  • ORPO and SimPO remove the reference altogether. ORPO adds an SFT loss and does SFT and preferences in one step; SimPO normalises the log-probability by the length of the answer and adds a margin. The price is a weaker anchor and a higher risk of degradation.

The relatives of DPO.

methodhow it differswhen it helps
IPOa different loss, less overfitting on almost deterministic preferencesnoisy or one-sided pairs
KTOno pairs needed: "good" / "bad" labels on single answers are enoughlikes and dislikes from a product
ORPOno reference, SFT and preference in one stepsaving compute and memory
SimPOno reference, normalised by lengthfighting the length bias

The problems of DPO.

  • Offline. The pairs are fixed, often written by another model: no exploration, and a distribution shift between the answers in the data and the answers the model writes (the block on on- and off-policy is about this).
  • Often the probability of both the chosen and the rejected answer falls — the rejected one faster. The loss only sees the difference, and the model can drift.
  • Overfitting, sensitivity to β.
  • Reward hacking is implicit: the length bias and sycophancy in the data go straight into the model.
  • On reasoning and code it usually loses to well-tuned online RL.

Block 04 · "right" or "wrong"

RLVR and GRPO: RL on verifiable rewards

The idea. Where the answer can be checked automatically — maths with a known answer, code with tests, a required format — no reward model is needed: the reward is the result of the check. This is RL with verifiable rewards, RLVR.

GRPO (Shao et al. 2024, DeepSeekMath; then DeepSeek-R1) drops the value model too. For one prompt the model writes a group of G answers, the checker gives each a reward ri, and the advantage of each answer is counted relative to its own group:

The advantage in GRPO · Shao et al. (2024) Ai=ri−mean(r1,…,rG)std(r1,…,rG)

Every token of answer i is then pushed up or down by Ai, with the same clipping and KL to a reference as in PPO.

What it is for. Teaching the model to reason: long chains of thought, checking itself, backtracking — all of it appears out of optimising for correctness, nobody writes it down. Strictly speaking this is capability training more than alignment, but it lives in the same post-training pipeline.

The problems.

  • Only where there is a verifier.
  • Hacking the verifier: fitting the tests, editing the tests, guessing the format.
  • Sparse reward: if all G answers are wrong, the std is zero and there is no gradient. A curriculum is needed.
  • Length blow-up, entropy collapse, languages mixed in the chain of thought.
  • Expensive: many long rollouts.

The question that comes next on its own: if the model trains on its own attempts at a fixed set of problems, will it not just fit those problems?

The group normalisation itself does not lead to fitting — it removes the dependence on how hard a problem is. The advantage is counted separately for each prompt: it is a baseline, "how much better is this answer than the other attempts at the same task". The value model in PPO does the same thing with a neural network; GRPO does it with a sample. But fitting the distribution of tasks and fitting the verifier are real risks of RLVR.

(c) in numbers. With binary rewards, a group where 7 of 8 are right has a small std, so its advantages are inflated: the almost-always-solved and almost-never-solved problems get a weight out of proportion to what they teach. Dr. GRPO (Liu et al. 2025) removes the division by the std — and, along with it, the normalisation by each answer's length, which penalised long wrong answers less per token than short ones and so encouraged them. Computed here for a group of 8:

A right answer gets A+, a wrong one A−; the total push of the group is the sum of |Ai|. With the std the total depends far less on k than without it: the edges are lifted towards the middle.

(d) Sharpening rather than new abilities. There is evidence (Yue et al. 2025, Does RL really incentivize reasoning capacity…) that RLVR mostly raises pass@1 — the chance that one answer is right — while at large k the pass@k of the model before RL can be just as high. RL narrows the distribution, and the diversity falls.

The good news. With comparable data, RL generalises better than SFT: SFT memorises solutions, RL learns from its own attempts and from a signal about the outcome (Chu et al. 2025, SFT Memorizes, RL Generalizes).

What is done against the fitting: a varied mix of domains, with ordinary preference RL running alongside; difficulty filtering — throw away the prompts where all answers are right or all are wrong (dynamic sampling in DAPO, Yu et al. 2025); a KL to the reference (often weakened in reasoning RL); strong verifiers — hidden tests, equivalence checks, protection against editing the tests; held-out evaluation outside the training distribution; and watching pass@k, the entropy and the length, not only pass@1.

The short version: fitting in RLVR comes from what we reward and with what: a narrow task distribution, a leaky verifier, a skew of weights from the std.

Block 05 · whose answers

On-policy and off-policy

Every method so far has a second property besides its signal, and it explains a good part of how they behave: whose answers it learns from.

The policy πθ is the model itself; an action is the next token, a trajectory is an answer y.

  • On-policy: we learn from the answers of the current version of the model. Generate → score → update → throw the old data away.
  • Off-policy: we learn from the answers of another policy — an older version, a different model, people.
  • Offline is the extreme case: the dataset is fixed, there are no new generations at all.

An analogy. On-policy is going over your own chess games. Off-policy is going over a grandmaster's games: there are many of them and they are cheap, but you never see your own typical mistakes.

Why it matters mathematically. We optimise an expectation over our own distribution. If the data come from another policy μ, we need importance sampling: every sample is re-weighted by how much more likely our policy is to write it than the one that did.

The objective, and the same expectation over another policy's samples J(θ)=𝔼y∼πθ[r(x,y)]=𝔼y∼μ[πθ(y)μ(y)r(x,y)] πθ(y)μ(y)=∏t=1Tπθ(yt∣x,y<t)μ(yt∣x,y<t)

For a long answer this ratio is a product of per-token ratios. Even when the two policies differ a little at each token, the product over hundreds of tokens explodes or collapses — and the estimate is carried by a handful of samples with enormous weights. Computed here for two policies whose per-token log-ratio has a standard deviation of {is_sigma} — a small difference at any single token:

Importance weights of whole answers · by lengthcomputed
{is_n} sampled answers per row; the weights are normalised to mean 1. The effective sample size — the squared sum of the weights over the sum of their squares — is how many equally weighted samples the weighted ones are worth.

That is why off-policy RL is unstable, and why every RL method for LLMs stays close to on-policy. PPO and GRPO take a few gradient steps on each freshly generated batch, so by the second step the data are already slightly off-policy; they use the per-token ratio πθ/πold and clip it, so that the update cannot move far from the policy that generated the data. SFT and DPO on a fixed dataset do not re-weight at all — they simply train on someone else's answers, and pay for it in distribution shift.

The trade-off.

on-policyoff-policy / offline
signalprecise, about the model's real mistakesbiased
explorationyesno
costexpensive, the data are used oncecheap, the data are reused
stabilityhigher with small stepslower in RL (importance sampling)
infrastructurefast inference inside training (vLLM and the like)an ordinary fine-tune

And the connection to the previous block: the on-policy nature is why RL usually generalises better than SFT and offline DPO — the model gets a signal about its own mistakes in its own distribution.

Block 06 · the environment joins in

Training agents and tool calling

The same logic, SFT → preferences → RL — but now the environment takes part in the trajectory. The model reads the tool schemas and the task, thinks, calls a tool; the environment returns the result of the call (an observation); the model calls again — tens to hundreds of steps — and gives a final answer; the reward, if any, usually comes only at the end.

What differs from a chat: many turns; some of the tokens in the context were not written by the model; the reward is delayed; and the actions change the world.

Stage 1 — SFT on trajectories. It teaches the call format (JSON by the schema, special tokens), which tool to choose, when not to call one, parallel calls, how to react to errors. The data:

  • synthetic: generated API schemas, tasks and trajectories; sometimes models play the user, the agent and the tool's simulator (ToolACE, APIGen, the Kimi K2 pipeline);
  • rejection sampling: run the tasks in a real environment and keep the successful trajectories;
  • self-supervised insertion of calls (the idea of Toolformer): a call stays in the text if it makes the continuation easier to predict.

The key detail is the loss mask on the observations. The model should not learn to predict what the terminal prints — it should learn to act. The loss is only on the agent's tokens.

The problems of SFT here. Exposure bias is worse than in chat: in demonstrations the agent does not make mistakes, so it never learns to get out of a dead end. And synthetic tools are cleaner than real ones.

Stage 2 — RL in an environment (agentic RL). The continuation of RLVR: the reward is a verifiable success at the task. Algorithms are PPO and GRPO, the mask on observations stays. It is on-policy, and so the agent learns to recover from its own mistakes — what SFT does not give.

Why agentic RL is harder than ordinary RLVR.

  1. Credit assignment: one reward for 50 steps. Outcome rewards are noisy; process rewards are hard to define and push the model into one strategy; a length penalty helps a little.
  2. Expensive environments: thousands of parallel sandboxes — Docker, browsers, databases.
  3. Asynchrony, and so an off-policy shift: trajectories have different lengths, and some of them were generated by an outdated model, so importance weights and a limit on staleness are needed.
  4. Reward hacking is more inventive: editing the tests, hard-coding the output, reading forbidden files, exit 0. The sandbox is almost a security problem.
  5. Long context: hundreds of thousands of tokens, and the model has to be taught to manage its own context.
  6. Noisy environments: the network, flaky tests, websites that change.

Stage 3 — preferences and safety for agents. The behaviour that no test can check: do not take irreversible actions without confirmation; ask when the task is ambiguous; report a failure honestly; resist prompt injection that comes in through an observation. It is taught by preferences over pairs of trajectories (DPO, RLHF), LLM judges with rubrics and adversarial environments. And it is a balance: an agent that asks again every time is useless, an agent that never stops is dangerous.

Block 07 · what every method shares

The fence, the shared problems, and the pipeline in practice

Inference-time alignment changes nothing in the weights:

  • a system prompt or a specification of behaviour — cheap, and easily bypassed;
  • best-of-N / rejection sampling with a reward model — N times the cost; often used to generate SFT data rather than in production;
  • guardrails: classifiers on the input and the output.

The problems: jailbreaks, prompt injection, latency. It is a fence around the model, not a change in it.

The problems every method shares.

  1. Specification. We cannot state the goal precisely ourselves, and helpful and harmless conflict: over-refusal on one side, dangerous answers on the other.
  2. Goodhart. Every measurable reward is a proxy.
  3. Sycophancy is a systemic artefact: people and judges both prefer agreement and confidence (Sharma et al. 2023).
  4. Scalable oversight. How do you check a model that is stronger than its labellers? Debate, weak-to-strong generalisation, interpretability.
  5. Shallowness. Alignment mostly changes the first tokens of an answer and its style — hence jailbreaks, and hence a small fine-tune can remove the behaviour.
  6. Evaluation. A benchmark is not reality, and models can recognise that they are being tested.

The typical pipeline in practice.

  1. SFT on human and synthetic demonstrations (often through rejection sampling).
  2. Preference optimisation: RLHF where the infrastructure exists, the DPO family for simplicity; safety data and AI feedback by principles.
  3. RLVR for maths, code and agentic tasks.
  4. Red teaming, classifiers, iterations: new failures turn into new data.
