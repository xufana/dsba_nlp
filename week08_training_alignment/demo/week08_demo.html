From Next Token to Assistant: What Each Training Stage Changes

Every model you actually talk to went through the same sequence: pretraining on trillions of tokens, and then a few rounds of much smaller training — supervised fine-tuning, preference tuning, reinforcement learning — that turn a text continuer into something that answers, refuses, and thinks before it answers.

We look at every round on one model: OLMo 2 1B, which Ai2 released after each stage, together with the data of each stage. Four checkpoints, one architecture. Like Pythia last week, that is an experiment somebody already paid for.

The second half is the question you will meet at work much sooner than the first one: if a few thousand examples can turn a base model into an assistant, why is "let's just fine-tune it" so often the wrong answer?

The claim for today ℒ(θ)=− ∑t∈trained tokens wtlog πθ(yt∣y<t)

Every stage is this one sum — week 2's next-token loss. What changes from stage to stage is only whose tokens are in the sum, and who decides the weight wt of each.

Read the picture top to bottom: the model writes more and more of its own training data, and a human — or a program — only says how good it was.

Block 01 · supervised fine-tuning

The same loss, with a mask

SFT is the cross-entropy of week 2 on dialogues, with one change. The model reads the whole conversation, but the loss is summed only over the tokens of the answer — every token of the prompt, the template's markers included, gets the label IGNORE = -100, the same value week 4 put on the padding and on the second pieces of words in NER. The model is not asked to predict what the user will write; it is asked to predict what the assistant writes, given everything before it.

The data is OLMo's own SFT mixture. One real dialogue, through SmolLM2-Instruct's template:

SFT · the loss and the gradient
The loss is born at the end: cross-entropy at the answer's positions only. The gradient goes back through the logits into every layer of the model — and also through the prompt's positions: they carry no loss, but the answer's tokens attended to them, so their hidden states are on the gradient's path. Every weight of πθ gets a gradient; nothing else is in memory.

Block 02 · preferences

  
From votes to Bradley–Terry to DPO

  

    
SFT has a limit that is built into its loss: it only ever says "write this". It never says "not that". And for most of what we want from an assistant — helpful but not wordy, confident but not invented, polite but not grovelling — it is hard to write the best answer, and easy to say which of two answers is better. So the next stage learns from comparisons. Let's make some.

  


  
Label it yourself

  

    
One prompt, four answers from four different models (UltraFeedback, Cui et al. 2023). Six pairs. For each pair the room votes — hands up for the left one, hands up for the right one — and the counts go into the table. Nobody sees the judge's scores or the models' names until the reveal.

Bradley–Terry, a step at a time

  

    
To learn from picks we need a model of how a pick happens; the one used is from 1952 — Bradley & Terry, ranking chess players and tournaments: every answer has a hidden score r, and only the difference of scores matters.

  

  
Bradley & Terry (1952)
    P(A≻B)=σ(rA−rB),
      ℒ=−∑voteslogσ(rwinner−rloser)
  

  

    
Fitting it to the votes is a few lines, and a step at a time it is readable: start every score at zero; then raise the score of whoever won more often than the model currently expects, lower the others, and repeat. Each step is one update of Hunter's (2004) iteration; the loss it lowers is the one the reward model will be trained with.

The reward model, PPO — and what DPO removed

  

    
A reward model is this loss on a network. InstructGPT (Ouyang et al. 2022), following Christiano et al. (2017): people pick between two answers; the SFT model with its vocabulary head swapped for one number is trained on the picks with -logσ(rchosen−rrejected) — logistic regression on the difference, the classifier applied to a pair. (Chatbot Arena's leaderboard is the same model fitted to votes over pairs of systems.)


Then, reinforcement learning. The SFT model generates, the reward model scores, and PPO (Schulman et al. 2017) pushes up the answers with high reward — with a penalty for drifting away from the SFT model, so that it does not find the reward model's blind spots. Count what PPO keeps in memory: the policy being trained, the frozen reference, the reward model, and a value network (critic) to estimate how good a half-written answer is — four models of the size of the policy, and generation inside the training loop. It worked; it was famously hard to make work.

  

  
RLHF's objective (Ouyang et al. 2022)
    maxπ𝔼y∼π[r(x,y)]−βKL(π(·∣x)∥πref(·∣x))

The reward model · trained on the pairs, then frozen

    

    
The reward model is trained first, on its own: the SFT body with the vocabulary head swapped for one number. The gradient of −logσ(rc−rr) flows into both of its forward passes — the chosen and the rejected answer go through the same weights. After this stage it is frozen.

  

  

    
PPO · the loss and the gradient

    

    
The reward is born outside the gradient: sampling a token is not differentiable, and the frozen reward model only hands over a number. That number, minus the KL to the reference, and the critic's values become the advantages Ât — constants for the loss. The gradient then enters πθ only through logπθ of the tokens it sampled itself, recomputed with gradients; the critic gets its own regression loss. Two models of four are trained.

  


  

    
Rafailov et al. (2023) noticed that the objective above has an exact solution. For a fixed reward, the best policy is the reference re-weighted by the exponentiated reward — and the reward can be read back off the policy. Z(x) is a sum over every possible answer; nobody can compute it. But both answers of a pair answer the same prompt, so βlogZ(x) appears in both scores and cancels in the difference. What is left contains only log-probabilities that we can compute.

  

  
Rafailov et al. (2023) · the reward read off the policy, and the loss
    r(x,y)=βlogπ*(y∣x)πref(y∣x)+βlogZ(x)
    ℒDPO=−logσ(β[
      logπ(yc∣x)πref(yc∣x)how much more likely the chosen answer became
      −
      logπ(yr∣x)πref(yr∣x)the same for the rejected one
      ])
  

  

    
No reward model, no sampling, no critic; two models in memory (the one trained and the frozen SFT), and a loss you can write in one line. The paper's subtitle says what happened: your language model is secretly a reward model — βlogπ/πref is the reward, and Bradley–Terry is what lets Z disappear. This is why the 2023–2025 open models, OLMo 2 among them, used DPO and not PPO for this stage.

  

  

    
DPO · the loss and the gradient

    

      

    
Four forward passes, one loss: logπ of the chosen and of the rejected answer under the model being trained and under the frozen reference. The reference's two numbers are constants — they can be computed once, before training. The gradient reaches πθ through both answers: up through the chosen one, down through the rejected one, weighted by σ(−margin).

  


  

    
The DPO loss, by hand

    

      

      

      

      

    

    
Move the four log-probabilities and β. The presets are the notebook's let's calculate: (b) the chosen answer went up and the rejected one down; (c) both went down, the rejected one faster. Same margin, same loss — and in (c) the chosen answer became e5≈150 times less likely. The loss only sees the difference. Whether that happens on a real model is a measurement.

  

  

    
Tülu 3's variant divides each log-ratio by the answer's length: Δw=2 over 100 tokens against Δl=1 over 10 tokens is a gap of +1 for DPO and −0.08 for length-normalised DPO — the pair flips. It is a different objective, not a numerical tweak.

Block 03 · RL with a verifier

  
The checker is the reward model

  

    
For one kind of task nobody has to compare anything: when the answer can be checked. A maths problem with a known number, code with tests, an output that must parse as JSON. Then the reward model is replaced by a program, and the reward cannot be fooled by style or length — only by the check itself. This is reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, RLVR (Lambert et al. 2024 named it for Tülu 3), and it is what OLMo's last stage did on maths problems. It is also how DeepSeek-R1 (2025) learned to reason.

  

  
GRPO: the group is the baseline

  

    
The algorithm that won is GRPO (Shao et al. 2024, DeepSeekMath). For one prompt the model writes a group of G answers; each gets its reward ri — 1 if the checker says right, 0 if not — and each answer's advantage is how much better it did than its own group. Then every token of answer i gets pushed up or down by Ai — the claim's wt — with the same KL leash to a reference as in PPO. Compare with PPO: no reward model (a checker), no critic (the group average is the baseline the critic used to estimate). Two of PPO's four models are gone.

  

  
Shao et al. (2024)
    Ai=ri−mean(r1,…,rG)std(r1,…,rG),
      wt=Ai for every token of answer i

GRPO · the loss and the gradient

    

      

    
Like PPO without the critic. The reward comes from a program — not differentiable, no weights; the advantages Ai are constants computed from the group. The gradient enters πθ only through logπθ of its own sampled tokens, every token of answer i with the same weight Ai; the frozen reference adds only the KL term (and is often dropped: Olmo 3's RL run sets β=0).

  


  
The checker

  

    
The whole "reward model" of RL with a verifier is fifteen lines of the notebook: cut off the base model's next Question:, look for \boxed{}, #### or the answer is, else take the last number in the text; 1 if it matches, 0 otherwise. Type an answer and see what it earns.

A reward for the format is a common helper — and the first thing a policy learns to farm. Move its weight: at 1.0 a wrong answer with the right tag earns as much as a right answer without it. Reward hacking in one slider; the verifier is a security boundary, not a grader.

  


  
Which prompts teach

  

    
GRPO learns only from the difference inside a group. If all G answers to a prompt get the same reward — all right or all wrong — every advantage is (ri−mean)/std=0, and the prompt contributes no gradient at all, although we paid for G generations.


So the question is: for a prompt the model solves with probability p, how often is a group mixed? All G right happens with probability pG, all wrong with (1−p)G, so a group teaches with probability 1−pG−(1−p)G. Too hard (p≈0): all wrong. Too easy (p≈1): all right. Only the middle teaches — which is why the RL datasets of 2025 are curated by difficulty, and DAPO drops the groups with no spread and samples new ones.

Thinking is written tokens

  

    
Thinking is written tokens. The other thing RL did in 2025 is to make models think out loud: given a reward only for the final answer and room to write, DeepSeek-R1-Zero learned by itself to write long chains of reasoning, to check, to backtrack — and its accuracy grew with the length of what it wrote. Most models of 2026 do this, and many — Qwen3, SmolLM3 — can do it or not, by one switch in the chat template: a pair of empty <think></think> tags. That is test-time compute: a third dial, next to week 6's N and D.

Block 04 · recipes

  
How the 2025–2026 recipes look whole

  
On-policy, off-policy

  

    
Now that we have seen all of them, one word sorts the methods — and the recipes below. Off-policy: the tokens in the sum were written by someone else — the web, a human, a teacher model, pairs collected last month. On-policy: the model writes them itself during training, something judges them, and the gradient goes back into the same model that wrote them.


SFT is off-policy by construction. DPO on a fixed dataset is off-policy too, even when the pairs were sampled from the SFT model before training — on-policy in a dataset's name says who wrote the answers, not who writes them at the step. PPO and GRPO are on-policy: a fresh sample every step. Rejection sampling — R1's third stage — is the hybrid: on-policy data, off-policy loss.

  

  

    
Where the tokens in the sum come from

    

    

  

  

    
Why it matters: the model that samples its own answers stays close to its own distribution, while SFT pulls it towards someone else's text, token by token. That is why RL moves the weights least of the three stages, and why RL forgets less than SFT at equal gain on the new task (Shenfeld et al. 2025, RL's Razor).

  


  
The recipes

  

    
Everyone does the same thing in a different order. Every model you talk to in 2026 went through an SFT stage, a preference stage and an RL stage; what differs is the data of each, and who writes the tokens — a human, a teacher model, or the model itself. That is the claim's table, read by rows. Switch the colouring.

  

  

    
Everyone does the same thing in a different order

    

    
Read the two colourings together: the type of a stage says which loss; who writes says whether the model is pulled towards somebody else's text (off-policy) or re-weighted on its own (on-policy). Every recipe ends on-policy. Small models get their reasoning from the top row's teachers, not from RL of their own.

  

  

    
"DeepSeek-R1" is usually told as "GRPO taught a model to reason". The paper has two models. R1-Zero is that story: RL with a checker straight on the base model, no SFT before it; it learned to write long chains of reasoning — in mixed languages, hard to read. R1, the released one, is the four-stage row above: a cold start (SFT on thousands of curated long solutions), RL with verifiable rewards plus a reward for keeping the reasoning in one language, rejection sampling — the model of stage 2 writes many solutions, only the verified ones are kept (about 600k), mixed with 200k of writing and general data, and the base model is fine-tuned on them again — and a final RL: checkers where an answer can be checked, preference models where it cannot. Stage 3 is the claim's table once more: SFT on the model's own samples, wt=1 on accepted answers and 0 on the rest — RL's data with SFT's loss.

  

  
Distillation

  

    
The same move is how small models get reasoning: distillation, a student trained on what a teacher produces. R1's distilled Qwen and Llama models are the first row of the table below — SFT on the 800 thousand samples, no RL stage; the paper reports that this beat running RL on the small model directly. Qwen3's small models take the first row and then the third. s1 (Muennighoff et al. 2025) took the first row to the extreme: 1 000 examples chosen from 59 000.

Distillation · the loss and the gradient

    

    
The teacher is frozen and only produces targets: its written answers (sequence distillation) or its distribution at every position (logit distillation). The gradient goes into the student only. In on-policy distillation the student writes the answers itself — the sampling is again outside the gradient — and the teacher scores every position of them.

Block 05 · the two questions from the top

  
Five things that transfer

  

    
What does each stage change? Whose tokens carry the loss and who weighs them. Pretraining: everybody's text, weighed equally — the knowledge. SFT: a written answer — the format. Preferences: a comparison — the style, the length, the refusals. RL with a verifier: the model's own answers, weighed by a checker — reliability on whatever can be checked, and the habit of thinking before answering. The architecture is the same; the weights move by a few percent.


When is fine-tuning the wrong move? When the problem is knowledge rather than format; when the abilities you are not measuring matter; when a prompt, a retrieved document or an encoder would do. It is always a trade: what the new data asks for, against everything it does not mention.
