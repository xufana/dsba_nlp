Author: Daria Andreeva

One model, five ways to teach it

In week 4 we froze BERT and turned one dial: the head only, the top blocks, everything. Today the body stays frozen and something small is trained next to it — a prompt, an adapter, a pair of thin matrices — and we ask how small it can be.

In this workshop, we will attempt to fine-tune the GPT2-small model for the task of sentiment analysis using various PEFT methods: LoRA, prompt tuning, and PEFT + LP-FT. To evaluate the quality of fine-tuning, we will use one of the GLUE benchmark datasets, SST-2 (Stanford Sentiment Treebank): sentences with movie reviews that need to be classified as positive or negative.

The question for today: how few parameters does a 124M model need to move to learn a new task — and what does moving fewer of them actually save?

Block 01 · head or body

Probing, fine-tuning, and both

A pretrained model learned a lot from a heap of data; a downstream task has little. Three ways to use it: train only the last layer — the head, linear probing; train the whole model, fine-tuning; or train a small set of weights next to the frozen body — parameter-efficient fine-tuning, the rest of today.

Probing is the simplest and fastest, and it does not change the information the model extracts. Fine-tuning is the longest and usually the most accurate: it teaches the model to extract what matters for the task. The question is what each does to data the task did not show — in distribution (ID: the test looks like the training set) against out of distribution (OOD: the same task, other data).

Why would training more hurt? Full fine-tuning starts with a random head. On the first steps its gradients are large and random, and they flow into the body: the features the model brought from pretraining get distorted before the head has learned what to read from them. The fix is two stages: first train the head alone, then fine-tune everything starting from that head — LP-FT.

The smallest network where this can happen: one feature z=b1x1+b2x2, one head weight v, the prediction vz. The pretrained feature is already right (b1=b2). The training data vary only along x1 (dots on the horizontal axis); the OOD data only along x2 (squares on the vertical axis). Train it three ways — this runs in your browser.

Two inputs, one feature, one head · trained in the pagepick a way to train
Fine-tuning from a random head reaches 100% on the training data. What happens to the OOD squares?
The gradient of b2 is v·x2·(…), and every training point has x2≈0: fine-tuning cannot touch b2. This random head started with the wrong sign, as half of random heads do; fine-tuning fixes the training data the fastest way — by flipping b1 — while b2 stays, so the boundary turns and every OOD square falls on the wrong side. Probing moves only v. LP-FT starts from the probed v, which already has the right sign: fine-tuning then stretches b1 in the direction it already had, and the boundary keeps pointing the right way.

Block 02 · train the prompt

Prompt tuning

Prompt tuning is based on the observation that large language models are capable of solving tasks in a zero-shot and few-shot paradigm, relying only on the input prompt. For models that can work in this format, prompt tuning suggests not selecting the prompt manually, but training it: a few vectors are put in front of the embeddings of the text, and only they — and, for classification, the head — are trained.

When configuring the prompt, you can vary its length as well as the initialization method: random, or the embeddings of a text prompt. The notebook starts from "Predict if sentiment of this review is positive or negative".

Block 03 · a small layer inside every block

Adapters

Adapters (Houlsby et al., 2019): after each attention and feed-forward layer a small trainable block is added — a skip connection around two fully connected layers that go down to a small dimension m (≈ 64) and back up. Only the adapters, the norms and the head are trained, and the quality reaches fine-tuning.

The cost is that the adapters are extra layers: they are computed after the layer they sit on, never in parallel with it.

Block 04 · a low-rank update

LoRA

When we fine-tune, every weight matrix W moves to W+ΔW. The idea of LoRA (Hu et al., 2021): ΔW does not need all of its numbers. Freeze W, and learn the update as the product of two thin matrices of rank r: B of size d×r and A of size r×k, with r around 8.

One LoRA layerafter Hu et al. (2021)
Step through the three states. At init B=0, so the model is exactly the pretrained one. In training only A and B get gradients. After training, merge: αrBA is added into W once, and inference costs nothing extra — unlike an adapter, there is no extra layer left.
The layer h= Wx+ αr BAx , A∼𝒩(0,σ2), B=0
Initialise, then train · a 16 × 16 layer in the page
A starts random and B at zero. What if both start at zero?
Both stay at zero forever: the gradient of B is proportional to Ax, the gradient of A to B⊤, and both are zero. One of them has to be random. B=0 is chosen so that training starts from the pretrained model exactly; random A with zero B trains as well as the reverse.

Why a low rank is enough

Why should a low rank be enough? The update is not the weights. A pretrained matrix uses all of its directions; the change a small task needs is a handful of them. Aghajanyan et al. (2020) measured how few directions a fine-tune needs: 200 trainable parameters, randomly projected back into the full space of RoBERTa, reach 90% of full fine-tuning on MRPC.

The most important parameter of this model is the rank of matrices A and B (r). Increasing it gives more degrees of freedom in adjusting the BA additive, thereby increasing the number of trainable parameters. The library is peft; the class below is what it does to one linear layer.

In the original article, the authors train only WQ and WV. GPT-2 computes Q, K and V with one layer, c_attn (768→2304), so target_modules=['c_attn'] gives one pair A,B for all three: the rank-8 update is shared by Q, K and V. What it does save is memory. Full fine-tuning with Adam keeps, for every parameter, the weight, its gradient and two optimizer moments — 16 bytes per parameter in fp32. LoRA keeps the frozen weights in 16 bits and the rest only for its few million. QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023) goes further: the frozen base is stored in 4 bits, enough to fine-tune a 65B model on a single 48 GB GPU.

What to train. A prompt, an adapter, the biases, or a low-rank update next to the frozen weights. LoRA wins on the combination: few parameters, no extra latency once merged, a tie with full fine-tuning.


What it saves. Not time per step — memory and storage. That is what makes fine-tuning a 7B model a notebook exercise, which is where week 8 starts.
